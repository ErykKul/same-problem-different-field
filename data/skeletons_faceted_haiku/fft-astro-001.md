MECHANISM: For a sequence of values observed at a set of possibly non-uniformly spaced sample points, a power quantity is computed as a function of a continuous frequency parameter. For each candidate frequency the observations are projected onto sine and cosine basis functions evaluated at the sample points, yielding two inner-product coefficients, and the power is their sum of squares normalized by the number of samples. When the sample points are equally spaced and the frequencies are the natural grid values, this reduces to the squared magnitude of a discrete linear transform of the sequence, computable by fast algorithms. For arbitrary sample points the power is instead obtained by solving, separately for each frequency, a least-squares fit of a single sinusoid, which produces a 2×2 linear system with a Gram matrix of the two basis vectors and a projection vector; the fitted coefficients are the matrix inverse applied to the vector and the associated power is the corresponding quadratic form. Because the two basis projections become correlated under irregular sampling, the matrix is decorrelated through a Cholesky factorization so that, under a zero-mean Gaussian white-noise null model, the normalized power follows an exponential distribution. From this null distribution a detection threshold is derived by fixing a false-alarm probability across an effective number of independent frequencies. Peaks of the power curve exceeding the threshold are taken as evidence of a periodic component. The mean of the sequence may be subtracted beforehand because under irregular sampling a nonzero mean contaminates the entire frequency axis rather than a single point. Closed-form expectations and variances of the coefficients are also derived, and the analysis shows that the highest distinguishable frequency depends on the arithmetic structure of the sample points rather than only on their average spacing.
