MECHANISM: A history-dependent stochastic process is studied in which the set of available outcomes shrinks as a sequence unfolds. From observed data a binary directed network is built, where an entry records whether one element can immediately succeed another, so each node carries an out-set of permitted successors. A single scalar summarizing the hierarchical nesting of these out-sets is defined as the average over all node pairs of the overlap of their out-sets normalized by the smaller set size, bounded between zero and one. Synthetic sequences are then generated by a forward simulation: pick a starting node uniformly, then repeatedly move to a uniformly chosen member of the current node's out-set for a fixed number of steps, and repeat over many independent runs to accumulate a large pool of outcomes. The rank-ordered frequency distribution of outcomes is computed and its decay exponent estimated by a least-squares fit over a rank window. The fitted exponents are compared across many datasets against the nesting scalar, and significance is assessed by correlation coefficients with p-values. Null models are constructed by permuting the network in two ways, one destroying nesting while preserving out-degrees and one preserving nesting while destroying succession order, plus a time-reversal control using the transposed network and a difference-in-means test. Analytically, the limiting outcome distribution under strict nesting is shown to follow an inverse-rank power law, with a sharp breakdown under weak nesting, and a parametrized family of synthetic networks maps the nesting strength to the resulting exponent.
