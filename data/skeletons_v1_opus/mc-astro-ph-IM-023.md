MECHANISM: The future trajectory of a moving body governed by a dynamical system is predicted by numerically integrating its averaged equations of motion forward in time. The state is reduced to slowly varying mean coordinates, and the dominant dissipative force is computed at each step by evaluating an environmental-density field along the orbit and combining it with a resistance coefficient and the squared relative speed; per-step effects are averaged over one revolution using fixed-node quadrature. Integration advances with an adaptive-step Runge-Kutta scheme, the step shrinking as the decay rate grows, until a terminal threshold on a state variable is crossed, yielding a predicted event time. A key unknown coefficient is first estimated from historical state observations by differencing successive observed states to recover the instantaneous deceleration, dividing by the density-weighted squared speed, and then filtering the resulting noisy per-interval estimates over a lookback window after excluding non-stationary segments. Predictive uncertainty is produced by Monte-Carlo dispersion: the effective force magnitude is multiplied by random scale factors drawn from a symmetric log-uniform law, an external driver is perturbed by normal noise, and an ensemble of trajectories is integrated. Each ensemble is scored against the realized event time by a probabilistic distance that generalizes mean absolute error, and the rank of the truth within the ensemble is computed and compared to a uniform reference via an integrated squared-discrepancy criterion; the dispersion magnitude is tuned to optimize this calibration, and metrics are bootstrap-averaged over many entities.
