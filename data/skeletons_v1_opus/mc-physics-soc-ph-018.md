MECHANISM: Over a network of binary-valued entities driven by a stochastic spreading process, a scalar aggregate is defined as a weighted sum of the entity states. The goal is to select a bounded-cost subset of entities whose observed states are maximally informative about this aggregate, formalized as maximizing the mutual information between the subset's states and the aggregate, equivalently minimizing the conditional entropy of the aggregate given the subset. The selection objective is shown to be intractable: it is NP-hard and even hard to approximate within a logarithmic factor, proved by reduction from a covering problem, and it is shown to be supermodular under independence rather than submodular, so greedy guarantees do not transfer. Exact evaluation requires an exponential sum, but is made tractable in special structures: when states are independent the residual aggregate follows a sum-of-independent-indicators distribution computable by direct convolution; on a tree the conditional distribution is obtained by contracting realized paths, pruning, and a message-passing recursion; on a chain a closed-form optimal spacing of selected entities is derived via a chain-rule entropy decomposition and a first-derivative condition. For general structures the objective is estimated from many simulated realizations of the spreading process using a plug-in empirical entropy, and entities are added one at a time by greedily maximizing estimated information gain. Complexity and sample-complexity bounds are given for each routine, and selected subsets are compared against degree- and frequency-based baselines.
