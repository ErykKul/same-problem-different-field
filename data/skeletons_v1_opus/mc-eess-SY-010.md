MECHANISM: A collection of moving entities, each with a position evolving under discrete-time dynamics, must arrange so that the empirical distribution of their final positions matches a prescribed target distribution given as a weighted point set. Discrepancy is measured by the squared quadratic transport distance, defined as the minimum over nonnegative coupling matrices, with row and column marginal constraints, of total squared-distance times transported mass; this is a linear program when positions are fixed but becomes nonconvex because positions are control-dependent. To make it tractable, the global coupling is decomposed per entity: with one entity's position fixed, its single-source allocation has a closed-form greedy solution that assigns its unit of mass to the nearest target points first, subtracting from each target's residual capacity, processed sequentially so allocations stay feasible and disjoint. Given its assigned targets, each entity steers toward the mass-weighted barycenter of those points; for linear dynamics the energy-minimal finite-horizon input that hits the barycenter is obtained in closed form via the pseudoinverse of the reachability map, equivalently the controllability Gramian, and for control-affine nonlinear dynamics via the necessary conditions of a costate recursion solving a two-point boundary value problem with a quadratic effort penalty. The select-then-steer cycle repeats over fixed horizons. A surrogate cost equal to the assigned transport cost is shown to be a strictly decreasing upper bound on the true transport distance across cycles, giving a cycle-wise descent guarantee. A memory-with-decay rule lets each entity reuse stale neighbor weights so the scheme runs under intermittent, range-limited communication. Behavior is verified in simulation against the transport-distance metric.
