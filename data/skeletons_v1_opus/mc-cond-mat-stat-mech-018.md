MECHANISM: A large collection of recorded spatial trajectories is analyzed to extract a scaling law of dispersal. Each trajectory is mapped from curved geodetic coordinates into a local flat Cartesian frame. For the planar component, a time-averaged second moment of displacement is computed as a function of lag, and its log-log slope yields a single scaling exponent characterizing how spread grows with time, revealing a faster-than-diffusive but slower-than-straight-line regime. To explain the exponent, each trajectory is treated as the observable output of a latent discrete-time process whose hidden state evolves as a finite Markov chain with a small number of regimes; simple binary and geometric features derived from the path serve as emissions assumed conditionally independent given the state. The latent-state model is calibrated to the data, and the most probable state sequence segments each path into distinct kinematic regimes. The same second-moment-versus-lag diagnostic is then recomputed conditionally within each regime, showing that one regime is straight-line, one is strongly sub-diffusive, and one contributes little net displacement. Sojourn-time distributions per regime are summarized by fitting heavy-tail exponents using a tail-based order-statistic estimator. Sub-populations are stratified by an experience proxy and the exponents are re-estimated to detect systematic shifts. The exponent is finally rationalized through autocorrelation and intermittent-relocation arguments, attributing the anomalous value to imperfectly persistent, two-dimensional, alternating motion.
