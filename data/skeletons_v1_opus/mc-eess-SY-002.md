MECHANISM: A system of interacting elements is represented as an undirected graph, and the question is the minimum number of special elements that must be driven or sensed for the whole configuration to be steerable or inferable. The graph is recast as a bipartite structure with each vertex duplicated into a source copy and a sink copy and an edge for every original link, and a maximum matching (largest edge set sharing no endpoints) is computed on it by a polynomial-time algorithm. Vertices left uncovered by the matching are identified as the required special elements, and their count is taken as the controllability/observability metric; equivalent unmatched sets, i.e. the slack directions, are extracted by a bipartite decomposition into well-matched, over-determined, and under-determined blocks. This structural count is related to the generic rank deficiency of the configuration matrix and shown to agree with the numerical rank deficiency for almost all parameter choices. To probe the effect of local cliquishness, ensembles of synthetic graphs are generated by growth with degree-proportional attachment, in one family with an extra step that closes triangles, holding the heavy-tailed degree profile and average degree fixed; the unmatched-vertex count and a triangle-density coefficient are averaged over many random realizations and compared across the two families. A constructive procedure then adds a few edges between successive vertices inside slack blocks to raise triangle density on real graphs, and the resulting drop in unmatched-vertex count is tabulated. The consistent finding is an inverse relation between triangle density and the number of required special elements.
