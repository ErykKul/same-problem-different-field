MECHANISM: An aggregate is grown on a two-dimensional regular lattice by an iterative stochastic rule: starting from a single occupied seed cell, walkers are released one at a time from a circle just outside the current occupied region and take unbiased discrete steps to neighboring cells until they land adjacent to an occupied cell, at which point they become permanently occupied; walkers straying beyond a cutoff radius are discarded. Repeating this attaches a large number of cells, producing a branched occupied set. The mass-radius scaling exponent is extracted by fitting a power law between a spread measure of the occupied set and the number of occupied cells, and equivalently by counting occupied boxes as a function of box size and taking the slope on logarithmic axes. To remove boundary effects, the spread measure for several lattice sizes is rescaled by a power of the lattice size and plotted against mass divided by lattice size raised to the scaling dimension, collapsing onto one curve and revealing a crossover. The attachment-probability field on a frozen occupied set is estimated by launching many additional walkers and recording first-contact sites; its multiscale character is summarized by a partition function of summed box probabilities raised to a power, from which a spectrum of scaling exponents is derived by transform. A spatial decay of contact probability with distance from the periphery is fit to an exponential. Branch lengths, junction angles, gap inhomogeneity, and path winding are measured from a thinned skeleton, and power-law fits use error-aware regression with uncertainties from repeated runs.
