MECHANISM: A collection of entities is summarized by coarse-grained effective interaction descriptors, each a point in an abstract space equipped with a distance satisfying positivity, symmetry, and the triangle inequality. This space is assumed to embed into a finite-dimensional metric manifold of bounded intrinsic dimension, and the relevant descriptors are confined to a bounded region, guaranteeing finite packing numbers. A macroscopic configuration is defined as a subset of this space whose elements all yield the same coarse pattern, and two configurations are called separable if the infimum distance between them exceeds a fixed positive threshold. The central argument reduces the question of how many distinct configurations can coexist to a geometric packing-and-separation problem in a bounded low-dimensional metric space. A theorem then establishes that the number of pairwise-separated configurations is bounded above by a quantity depending only on the intrinsic dimension, the separation threshold, and the metric, independent of the underlying microscopic size. A corollary states the impossibility of unbounded distinct configurations without raising the dimension. Extensions show the bound persists when the metric varies slowly in time under a Lipschitz condition, and when an asymmetric distance is symmetrized by taking the maximum of the two directed distances. Proofs invoke standard packing and metric-geometry results. The conclusions are framed as impossibility statements rather than constructive procedures.
