MECHANISM: Given a set of observations of a quantity recorded at a set of sample positions along an axis, the goal is to detect and quantify oscillatory components hidden in noise. One forms, over a grid of candidate oscillation rates, a coefficient pair by correlating the observations against a cosine and a sine basis function evaluated at the sample positions, then combines the squared coefficients into a power score per candidate rate. For evenly spaced samples this reduces to the squared magnitude of a sum of complex exponentials, and the basis is orthogonal so the scores are independent. For uneven spacing the basis is no longer orthogonal, so the power is instead defined through a small least-squares fit: at each candidate rate one solves a two-by-two linear system whose matrix is the Gram matrix of the cosine and sine vectors and whose right side is their inner products with the data, yielding a quadratic form in the solution. Under a zero-mean noise hypothesis the squared coefficients are decorrelated by a triangular factorization of the Gram matrix, giving each score an exponential reference distribution. A detection threshold is set by inverting the probability that the maximum over many independent candidates exceeds a level. To restore a fast transform one snaps the irregular sample positions to the nearest cells of a dense regular grid, zero-filling empty cells, and a first-order expansion bounds the rebinning error as proportional to the rate times the cell width. The mean of the data is subtracted first to avoid spurious cross-correlation between the two coefficients.
