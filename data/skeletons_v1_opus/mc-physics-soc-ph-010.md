MECHANISM: A fixed directed graph carries conserved commodities routed from source vertices to sink vertices, with each vertex assigned an independent random weight drawn from a heavy-tailed distribution whose survival function decays as a power law. Sink demands are proportional to vertex weights, source outputs are fixed by a function of demands subject to balance, production-capacity, and edge-flow-capacity constraints, and the flow on edges is determined by solving a parametric convex optimization that enforces conservation at every vertex. A discrete-time stochastic failure process is then run: an initial random set of edges fails, overloaded edges (flow exceeding capacity) fail probabilistically as partial capacity reductions or full removals, disconnected components are rebalanced by proportional scaling, flows are recomputed by re-solving the optimization, and the process iterates until no further failures occur. A scalar cost summarizes the disruption after termination. Under mild scale-invariance and one-sided continuity assumptions on the cost and on the conditional failure probabilities, an extreme-value argument shows the dominant cause of a large cost is a single vertex weight far exceeding all others (a catastrophe principle), bounding the off-diagonal contribution. From this, the cost's tail is proven to inherit a power law whose exponent is the input exponent divided by the cost's homogeneity degree, with an explicit leading constant expressed through single-active-vertex cost evaluations. Sufficient conditions on the modeling choices are derived inductively via transformations of the optimization and compactness-based continuity arguments, and verified on several concrete instantiations.
