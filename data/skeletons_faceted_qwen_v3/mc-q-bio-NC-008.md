MECHANISM: The paper argues that biological constraints on neural networks, such as nonnegative firing rates and energy efficiency, break symmetries in connectionist models, leading to interpretable single-neuron responses that align with specific algorithms. It suggests that these constraints, when combined with task requirements, narrow the space of plausible algorithms to those resembling biological implementations. The paper reviews examples where simplified biological details (e.g., no glia, no dendrites) produce models that replicate brain responses, such as grid cell hexagonality or dopamine-based reinforcement learning. It contrasts this with artificial neural networks (ANNs), noting that while ANNs can solve tasks with diverse algorithms, biological constraints favor solutions mirroring neural data. The paper posits that single-neuron tuning, shaped by these constraints, can diagnose the underlying algorithm, as seen in cases like XOR task solutions differing based on energy costs. It emphasizes that understanding the brain’s algorithm requires integrating top-down task constraints, middle-level neural architecture properties, and bottom-up biological details that break symmetries. The discussion highlights that while some biological features (e.g., ion channels, spiking dynamics) may not constrain algorithms, others (e.g., nonnegativity, energy efficiency) are critical for aligning models with neural data. The paper concludes that computational neuroscience should prioritize incorporating these constraints into models to better infer brain algorithms from neural responses.
