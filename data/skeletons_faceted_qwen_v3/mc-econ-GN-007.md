MECHANISM: The paper models strategic expression in social media through a utility function that agents optimize to balance popularity gains against alignment/misalignment costs. Agents hold fixed authentic opinions and derive utility from the popularity of their posts (measured by likes) and from exposure to content that aligns with their authentic views, while incurring losses from misaligned content. Social media interaction is modeled as a sequential process where posts are shown to followers, who then decide to like or not like them based on alignment with their own opinions. The equilibrium concept is Subgame Perfect Nash Equilibrium, where agents simultaneously choose posts to maximize their utility, considering the visibility of their posts to different opinion groups and the resulting likes. The model shows that popularity incentives can distort the frequency of existing opinions, amplifying polarization in polarized societies and dampening it in unified ones. A key finding is the "popularity trap," where individually optimal strategic posting leads to the disappearance of authentic opinions, worsening welfare. The analysis uses a three-opinion space with intensity parameters to study state-dependent effects on polarization and welfare, comparing outcomes to benchmarks like autarky and truthful expression. The utility function incorporates parameters for popularity, alignment, and misalignment, with equilibrium posts constrained to existing opinion sets due to the structure of the utility maximization problem.
