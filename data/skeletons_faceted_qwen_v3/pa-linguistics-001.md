MECHANISM: The paper computes a model where a sequence of nested sample spaces is generated through a transition matrix, simulating sentence formation by iteratively selecting words from progressively restricted subsets. The process begins with a vocabulary of N words, represented as a matrix where each row corresponds to a word and contains the set of words that can follow it. A sentence is constructed by starting with a random word, then selecting subsequent words from the subset defined by the current word's row in the matrix. This creates a nested sequence of sample spaces, reducing the available options at each step. The model quantifies nestedness as the average overlap between subsets of words, calculated using the formula n(M) = ⟨|Ω_i ∩ Ω_j| / min(|Ω_i|, |Ω_j|)⟩ over all word pairs. Simulated sentences are generated by repeatedly applying this selection process, and the resulting word frequency distributions are compared to empirical data. The model demonstrates that nestedness in the transition matrix directly influences the power-law exponent of word frequencies, explaining Zipf's law without assuming preferential attachment or self-organized criticality. Deviations from strict nesting lead to variations in the scaling exponent, and the model's predictions are validated against real texts by comparing simulated and observed frequency distributions. The analysis shows that destroying nestedness (via random permutations of the matrix) eliminates the power-law behavior, confirming the model's reliance on hierarchical structure. The computational steps include matrix construction, iterative sampling, frequency counting, and statistical comparison of exponents between simulated and empirical data.
